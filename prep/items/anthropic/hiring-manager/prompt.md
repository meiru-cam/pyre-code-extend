A *hiring manager* is the manager of the specific team a role would join, distinct from the recruiter who runs the process and from the engineers who run the technical rounds. An *individual contributor* (IC) is an engineering role without direct reports, as distinct from an engineering-manager role; several prompts below vary by this distinction and by seniority. This is a one-on-one, conversational round with the hiring manager, scheduled for 45 to 55 minutes; the manager moves between the groups below depending on your background rather than following a fixed script, and the round can end once its ground is covered, sometimes well before the scheduled time. It can sit at different points in a loop: as a phone screen ahead of the onsite, or as the last round, scheduled after the onsite's coding, system-design and culture rounds; placed last, it also functions as a check of fit between your background and the team's current direction, and a loop can end there even when every earlier round went well. The manager's own register ranges from warm and exploratory to reserved and procedural; the six groups below are what the round covers under either register. Bring, for each group, a story with your own specific role and a result you can state as a number or an observable change; where the loop also includes a separate project deep-dive presentation, keep the project, the numbers and the decisions you describe consistent between the two rounds.

### Past impact

- Walk through the project you consider your most impactful, and state specifically what you personally did in it, separate from what the team around you did.
- Describe the most complex project you have shipped: a difficulty you anticipated going in, and one that only became visible once the work was already underway.
- For senior ICs and staff-level candidates: describe how your impact reaches beyond the team you sit on directly.

### Prioritisation and ownership

- Describe the framework you use to decide between technical debt and feature work when both compete for the same engineering time.
- State whether a project you led carried a hard deadline, and describe how that deadline shaped the decisions you made.
- Describe a piece of work you deliberately chose not to do, or chose to cut, and describe a separate time you took on a piece of work outside the scope you were formally assigned.
- Describe a project that failed to reach its goal: its actual impact, what you learned from it, and what you would do differently if you ran it again.

### Collaboration

- Describe a project where you worked closely with a partner outside engineering — product, research, policy, design, data, or another non-engineering function.
- Describe a cross-functional collaboration that did not go well, and how it was resolved.
- Describe a disagreement with a peer or with your own manager, and how it was settled.
- Describe a time you held a view different from someone else's and persuaded them by changing your own approach, rather than by repeating your original argument.
- Describe a time you influenced a roadmap or a set of priorities that you did not own; for individual contributors, this can be a time you led an effort without formal authority over the people involved in it.

### Growth and mentorship

- Describe someone you helped grow, and the outcome for them.
- Describe a piece of feedback you received that changed how you work.
- Describe how you get up to speed in an area that is new to you, quickly.

### Team fit and working style

- Give a short account of your current work, and how you want your career to develop over the next few years.
- State what kind of team and what kind of problems you are looking for next, and why.
- Describe how you operate when a piece of work has no clear scope yet, or the goal itself is still unsettled.
- Describe how much you validate by default before moving to the next step of a piece of work, and how that changes under a deadline.
- For senior ICs and engineering managers: state the scope, the headcount and the objectives and key results (OKRs) behind a leadership claim you make elsewhere in the round, and describe a conflict you had with a peer leader.

### Your questions for the manager

- The round reserves time near its end for you to ask the hiring manager questions of your own about the team, the role, or how the group works.
- This is the one part of the round where the direction of the conversation is yours to set.
